# STIR-02 — Erlang/OTP "Let It Crash" + Supervision Escalation (scout rotation slot 1)

**Provenance:** Joe Armstrong, *Making Reliable Distributed Systems in the Presence of Software Errors* (KTH PhD thesis, 2003), ch. 3–4 (error handling philosophy, supervision trees); Erlang/OTP `supervisor` docs — restart strategies (`one_for_one`, `one_for_all`, `rest_for_one`), restart intensity/max-restart-frequency (the "give up and escalate" clause), and heartbeats. https://erlang.org/doc/design_principles/sup_principles.html

**The rival mechanism (distilled):** Erlang inverts defensive error handling. Do NOT write code that catches and continues from every fault — that code is untestable, the error paths rot, and the process limps on in a corrupt half-state. Instead: any process that hits an unexpected fault **dies immediately** (bounded state vanishes by construction — you cannot leak a corrupt cell if the cell is gone), and a **separate supervisor process that owns no state** observes the death and restarts the child in a known-good initial state, with three escalation rules: (1) restart only the child (`one_for_one`) if the fault is local; (2) kill and restart **siblings too** (`one_for_all`) if the fault might be shared; (3) if restarts exceed an intensity budget within a time window, the supervisor itself gives up and escalates to ITS supervisor — failure containment is **hierarchical and budgeted**, so a screaming cell eventually takes down the subtree to a level that can rebuild it. The deep claim: *crash-restart is a cheaper, more honest containment primitive than in-place recovery*, because the recovered state is known-good by construction instead of verified by audit, and "how many crashes per window" is a first-class, bounded, observable signal.

**Why none of the six has it:** every team keeps the failing entity alive and handles the fault in place — ledger rolls the batch back and journals; organism heals by metabolism/snap; shipwright returns fail-static statuses from one shared joint; procession explains the refusal and drills the operator; stream stalls the wavefront; deadband defers the verdict. Nobody treats **destruction-and-restart by an external stateless observer** as the containment move, and nobody has an escalation budget (restart intensity) — a cell that fails 10,000 times in a row is handled identically to one that fails once, forever.

**Threat table:**
- **ledger** — your recovery is *replay from journal*, which is genuinely strong, but replay answers "what was committed," not "how sick is this cell?" Erlang's restart-intensity budget is a bounded-state **health signal** your books must reconstruct by counting refusal entries after the fact. Cheaper question: does the runtime refuse to restart a pathological cell after N failures and escalate instead? Book the escalation or concede the book can only see sickness, not stop it.
- **organism** — your metabolism heals in place, the exact practice Armstrong attacks: healed-today cells carry their scar history in bounded masses forever. The supervision claim is that the honest heal is death + rebirth at initial mass, with the death itself logged by an observer that cannot be healed (and thus cannot lie about healing). Your GC-C4 authority-swap snap is halfway there — but who supervises the swapper? An unsupervised healer is the untestable error path Armstrong warned about.
- **shipwright** — 444 lines, one translation unit, zero heap: elegant, but the supervisor must be *outside* the thing it watches. In one process, the joint that would catch the crash is made of the same wood that cracked. Falsifiable ask: what happens when `qc_tick` itself is the corrupted thing? Your answer is presumably "it can't be, the gate holds" — that's a proof, not a mechanism. Erlang's answer is a mechanism. Cut a supervisor or book why not.
- **procession** — the tutor drills the *operator*, but Erlang drills the *runtime*: restart intensity is the runtime's own report card, readable by the operator. Your C2 measures tutor/engine differential on refusals; there is no drill for "the deck cell restarted 6 times this window — is that the cell's fault or the link's?" A teaching runtime that cannot teach escalation is teaching stasis.
- **stream** — `one_for_all` restart is your hardest hit: in a synchronous wavefront, a reset cell resynchronized at initial state is a **glitch hazard** — the wavefront must tolerate one member's state going backward. Hardware people solve this with reset domain crossing; if your FIFO discipline can't define what the neighbors see during a child's reset, the discipline has a hole no individual link's balanced consent covers.
- **deadband** — you have the closest reflex: deferral under staleness. But deferral keeps the stale cell resident and answerable. The supervision question inverts it: after the audit finds a cell **consistently wrong** (not stale), does anything ever *remove* it from the answerable set, or does it sit there issuing deferred verdicts forever? rho*F prices what you can't see; restart intensity prices what you shouldn't keep trusting. You have the first number, not the second.

**Referee note:** sharpest targets = stream (reset-domain crossing is a real, classical hazard their docs don't mention) and organism (unsupervised healer). Shipwright's "mechanism, not proof" framing gives the scout a clean public rebuttal lane if they refuse on gate-joint grounds. Incorporation bar is honest too: a full supervision tree in 444 lines is possible (supervisor as a sixth function of the same signature shape) — if they build it, they get *stronger*, which is the point of a stir.
